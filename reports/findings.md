# Findings

Every number below was produced by `scripts/generate_reports.py` against the real
Olist dataset and is stored verbatim in `reports/findings_metrics.json`. If you change
the pipeline, re-run that script — do not edit this file by hand.

**Eligible population:** 96,478 delivered orders · 93,358 distinct customers ·
15 Sep 2016 → 29 Aug 2018 · recency measured to 29 Aug 2018.

Each finding follows the same structure: *business question → what the data shows →
why it matters for a marketing decision → alternative explanations.*

---

## F1. What proportion of customers place repeat orders?

**Observed.** **3.00%** of customers (2,801 of 93,358) placed at least two eligible
orders. 90,557 customers bought exactly once. Orders per customer: **1.033**.

**Why it matters.** This is the base rate that any retention programme operates on.
With 97% one-time buyers, a campaign that raises the repeat rate by even 0.3
percentage points moves roughly 280 customers — meaningful in absolute terms, but any
business case must be built on measured incremental lift, not on an assumed "typical"
retention improvement.

**Alternatives to consider.** The dataset spans a growing marketplace; many customers
first bought late in the window (12,971 customers are within 60 days of the analysis
date and cannot yet be observed as repeaters). The true long-run repeat rate is
therefore somewhat higher than 3% — but the observed ceiling of how many *could* have
repeated in 12 months is 2.91% median across mature cohorts, so the correction is small.

---

## F2. How does retention vary across first-purchase cohorts?

**Observed.** Pooled **month-1 retention 0.48%**, month-2 **0.34%**, month-12
**0.17%**. Median 12-month cumulative repeat rate across the 11 mature cohorts:
**2.91%**. 23 cohorts exist; 11 are mature enough to carry a 12-month figure.

| Months since first purchase | Pooled retention |
|---|---|
| 1 | 0.48% |
| 2 | 0.34% |
| 3 | 0.25% |
| 6 | 0.23% |
| 9 | 0.16% |
| 12 | 0.17% |

**Why it matters.** Month-level retention is *very* low, and it does not decay to zero
in a smooth curve — it is flat and noisy, which is what a "random small trickle of
returning customers" looks like rather than a structured retention process. It also
means the difference between good and bad cohorts is mostly noise at this scale;
cohort-date personalisation would be chasing noise.

**Alternatives to consider.** Specific-month retention understates relationship
strength for a retailer whose natural repurchase cycle looks annual. The cumulative
measure (2.91%) is the fairer summary of "did they ever come back". Both are reported
separately in the app so neither is misread.

## F3. How long does it take a customer to order again?

**Observed.** Among repeaters, median time from first to second order **29 days**
(p75 = 126 days). Across *all* observed repeat gaps: median **29.5 days**, **50.1%
within 30 days**, **68.9% within 90 days**; 75th percentile 121.5 days, 90th
percentile 240.3 days.

**Why it matters.** This is the most actionable number in the project. Half of all
repeat business happens inside a month. Onboarding and post-purchase communication
should be front-loaded into the first 30–45 days; a win-back email sent at month 6 is
aimed at a period in which almost nothing normally happens.

**Alternatives to consider.** The gap distribution is computed on *observed* repeaters
only, so it says nothing about customers who never repeat. Timing recommendations
should be validated with a randomised test before being locked into automation.

## F4. Which customers have the highest historical monetary value, and how
   concentrated is it?

**Observed.** Top 1% of customers hold **11.5%** of historical merchandise value; top
10% hold **41.1%**; top 20% hold **56.6%**. Gini coefficient **0.52**. Mean value per
customer R$ 141.62; average order value R$ 137.04 (median R$ 86.58).

**Why it matters.** Value is concentrated, but **not** in the 80/20 pattern usually
quoted: the top 20% generate 57% of revenue, which leaves 43% spread across the
long tail of one-time buyers. A VIP-only strategy addresses less than half of
historical value, so both "protect the top" and "convert the middle" have commercial
cases — and the second is larger in absolute currency.

**Alternatives to consider.** Historical value mixes category mix and price level.
A customer buying one R$ 800 item is not necessarily a more valuable relationship than
one buying three R$ 150 items; frequency and margin-per-order would separate these,
and margin does not exist in this dataset.

## F5. Which customers are purchasing frequently, occasionally or infrequently —
   and which have not purchased recently?

**Observed (rule-based RFM, data-derived thresholds).**

| Segment | Customers | Share of customers | Share of value | Avg recency (days) | Avg orders | Avg order value |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| High-Value Active Repeat | 460 | 0.49% | 1.44% | 62 | 2.25 | R$ 183.83 |
| Active Repeat | 385 | 0.41% | 0.32% | 62 | 2.03 | R$ 53.90 |
| Lapsing Repeat | 840 | 0.90% | 1.63% | 182 | 2.12 | R$ 120.78 |
| Recent One-Time Buyer | 12,427 | 13.3% | 13.5% | 92 | 1.00 | R$ 143.31 |
| Dormant One-Time Buyer | 25,529 | 27.3% | 26.0% | 181 | 1.00 | R$ 134.84 |
| Long Inactive | 41,746 | 44.7% | 44.7% | 380 | 1.03 | R$ 137.67 |
| New / Insufficient History | 11,971 | 12.8% | 12.4% | 30 | 1.00 | R$ 136.77 |

Thresholds: lapsing = 121.5 days, inactive = 240.3 days, high value ≥ p80 of
historical value.

**Why it matters.** The "valuable repeat" segments together are only **1,685 customers
(1.8% of the base)** and **3.4% of historical value**. That is the opposite of the
usual enterprise assumption that a loyal core carries the P&L. Here the loyal core is
tiny: retention strategy cannot be a VIP-protection programme, because there is
almost no VIP tier to protect.

**Alternatives to consider.** Segments are a snapshot at one date; some "Long Inactive"
customers are seasonal or single-occasion buyers rather than lost relationships. The
boundaries are soft — a customer one day either side of 240.3 days changes label.

## F6. Are there behavioural differences between customer groups?

**Observed.** Repeat customers differ sharply from one-time buyers on recency, not on
basket size: average order value is roughly comparable across one-time segments
(R$ 134–143), while the repeat segments split internally into a high-value group
(R$ 184 per order) and a lower-value group (R$ 54). Repeat buyers also show a
bimodal timing pattern — 50.1% of repeat gaps within 30 days, but a long tail out to
240 days.

**Why it matters.** Because basket size does not separate repeaters from one-time
buyers, "spend more to find high-value customers" is not obviously the right targeting
rule. Timing appears to be the more discriminating dimension than value.

**Alternatives to consider.** This is descriptive co-variation; category mix (a
customer buying furniture has a different natural cycle than one buying cosmetics) is
a plausible driver of both recency and repeat behaviour and is only partially observed
here (dominant category per order).

## F7. What patterns are visible in reviews and delivery experience?

**Observed.** Review coverage **99.3%** of eligible orders; mean score **4.16**;
59.2% are 5s; **12.8%** are 1–2. Median delivery **10.2 days** (p90 = 23.1 days);
**8.1%** of orders arrive after the estimated date, and when late the median lateness
is **5.8 days**.

Delivery timing and satisfaction are strongly related:

| Delivery vs estimate | Orders | Mean review score | Share of 1–2 scores |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early | 86,713 | 4.30 | 9.2% |
| On time | 1,450 | 4.16 | 11.0% |
| 1–3 days late | 2,636 | 3.77 | 19.1% |
| 4–7 days late | 1,773 | 2.32 | 61.3% |
| 8+ days late | 3,252 | 1.73 | 78.4% |

**Why it matters.** Delivery-promise accuracy is the single largest controllable
driver of satisfaction visible in this dataset. Operations and marketing should be
treated as one system: a retention discount offered after a late delivery is paying to
compensate for an avoidable failure.

**Alternatives to consider.** Lateness is not randomly assigned — remote regions and
heavy/fragile categories are both harder to deliver and more likely to disappoint.
The gradient is consistent with a causal effect but does not isolate one.

## F8. Do service experience or review scores predict later purchasing?

**Observed — and this is the surprise.** No practically meaningful association.

- Repeat-within-90-days by review band: 1–2 → **2.41%**, 3 → **2.40%**, 4 → **2.18%**,
  5 → **2.60%** (n = 77,174; χ² p = 0.03; **Cramér's V = 0.011**).
- Repeat-within-90-days by lateness: Early 2.51%, On time 2.21%, 1–3 days late 2.44%,
  4–7 days late 1.83%, 8+ days late 2.30% (n = 77,735; **p = 0.45**, V = 0.007).
- 18,741 orders were right-censored and excluded; base rate over the 77,737 fully
  observed orders: **2.3%**.

**Why it matters.** The intuitive retention story — "bad delivery → bad review →
customer never returns" — is **not supported at the retention step**. Service quality
clearly drives *satisfaction* (F7) but does not measurably change *whether the customer
orders again* in this dataset. This inverts a common marketing assumption and is the
kind of result worth putting in front of a stakeholder: fixing delivery is still right
on satisfaction and brand grounds, but it should not be sold internally as a retention
lever without an experiment.

**Alternatives to consider (all plausible, none testable here).**
1. The effect is real but small, and 90 days is the wrong horizon.
2. Service problems matter through *composition*: they may destroy high-intent
   customers while being masked by the 97% who were never going to return.
3. Review submission is itself selective (99.3% coverage but non-random).
4. With a 2.3% base rate, an absolute effect of 0.2pp is within sampling noise, so the
   study is underpowered for small true effects — the correct conclusion is "not
   measurable here", not "no effect exists".

## F9. Which segments should a marketing manager investigate or target differently?

Derived from F1–F8 by `src/recommendations.py`; every evidence line is generated from
the computed profile:

| Priority | Segment | Why it is different | Action to test |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | Lapsing Repeat (840) | Proven repeaters now 182 days quiet; still 1.6% of value | Behaviour-based re-engagement, incentive only for non-responders |
| High | Recent One-Time Buyer (12,427) | 13.3% of customers, inside the 29-day median repeat window | Onboarding sequence timed to the observed gap |
| High | Service experience (all) | Lateness destroys satisfaction (F7) even though it shows no retention effect (F8) | Fix promise accuracy before discounting |
| Medium | High-Value Active Repeat (460) | Highest AOV (R$ 184), recent, but only 460 people | Service-led loyalty, cross-sell, no item-level discounting |
| Medium | Dormant One-Time Buyer (25,529) | Largest convertible pool; 26% of value, zero repeat | Content-led win-back prioritised by category and review score |
| Low | Long Inactive (41,746) | 380 days quiet; 95%+ never repeated | Low-cost channels only, budget-capped pilot |
| Low | New / Insufficient History (11,971) | Under 60 days old, one order | Not an audience — a measurement control |

**Why it matters.** The priority ordering follows measured signal strength, not
intuition: the biggest population (Long Inactive, 44.7% of customers) is explicitly
*not* the highest priority, because they hold no demonstrated repeat behaviour. That
is a decision the data supports.

**Alternatives to consider.** Priority also depends on contact costs and margins,
neither of which exists in the dataset. If acquiring a new customer is expensive,
the calculus shifts toward the dormant pool; the simulator exists so that assumption
can be made explicit rather than hidden.

## F10. Which additional data would be needed to estimate campaign profitability or
    prove that retention interventions work?

1. **Campaign exposure and contact history** — without it, no incremental effect can
   be attributed; every observed "reactivation" is confounded with seasonality.
2. **Contribution margin (or COGS) per order/category** — required to turn revenue
   into profit; currently all value figures are revenue-like.
3. **Acquisition cost and channel** — required for CAC payback and any credible CLV.
4. **A hold-out control group** — the only way to estimate incremental uplift.
5. **Returns, cancellations and complaint outcomes** — to separate service failure
   from demand loss.
6. **A longer observation window** — to mature recent cohorts and reduce censoring.
7. **Customer-level contact permissions and unsubscribe events** — to model contact
   fatigue and the cost of over-messaging.

**Why it matters.** Items 1 and 4 are the difference between a descriptive study and a
measurable marketing programme. Everything in this project is built so that those two
inputs could be added without re-architecting the pipeline.
